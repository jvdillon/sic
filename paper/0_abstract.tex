Biological neural systems must be fast but are energy-constrained. Winner-take-all circuits and time-to-first-spike coding---well-documented mechanisms in neural computation---implicitly treat \emph{when} a neuron fires as an expression of confidence. Inspired by this principle, we ask: can inference speed serve as a confidence signal for neural network ensembles?

We apply this principle to Tiny Recursive Models (TRM)~\citep{jolicoeur2025trm}. On Sudoku-Extreme, a baseline TRM achieves 85.5\% $\pm$ 1.3\%. But a key diagnostic reveals untapped potential: 89\% of failures are \emph{selection} problems---the model can solve these puzzles with a different random initialization. The true capability ceiling is 99\%, not 86\%.

Halt-first ensembling unlocks this potential: by selecting the first model to halt, we achieve 97\% accuracy vs.\ 91\% for probability averaging---while requiring 10$\times$ fewer reasoning steps. But can we internalize this as a training-only cost? Yes: by maintaining $K{=}4$ parallel latent states and backpropping only through the lowest-loss ``winner,'' we achieve \textbf{96.9\% $\pm$ 0.6\%} accuracy---matching ensemble performance at 1$\times$ inference cost.

As in nature, this work was also resource constrained: all experiments used a single RTX 5090. A modified SwiGLU~\citep{shazeer2020swiglu} made Muon~\citep{jordan2024muon} and high LR viable, enabling baseline training in 48 minutes and full WTA ($K{=}4$) in 6 hours---compared to TRM's 20 hours on an L40S.
